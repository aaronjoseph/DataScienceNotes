The current ratio, also called the working capital ratio, is a liquidity ratio: it measures whether a company's [[Current Asset|current assets]] can cover its [[Current Liability|current liabilities]], the obligations due within a year.

## Formula

$$
\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
$$

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** current assets 500 and current liabilities 400.

**Step 1: current ratio.**

$$
\frac{500}{400} = 1.25
$$

The company has 1.25 of short-term assets for every 1 of short-term obligations. Part of those assets may be slow-moving inventory, so a stricter test excludes it.

## Interpretation

- A ratio above 1 is generally preferred.
- Some strong companies run below 1. Generous dividends and share buybacks deplete current assets, and such companies can borrow through commercial paper when they need cash.
- A very high ratio can mean idle cash or excess inventory rather than strength.
- Piotroski's F-score treats a rising current ratio as a positive signal; see [[Piotroski Score]].
