Quantitative easing (QE) is a central-bank policy of buying large amounts of longer-term bonds, mainly government bonds, to push down long-term interest rates and support spending. Central banks use it when the short-term policy rate is already very low and cannot usefully be cut further.[^boe-qe]

## Background: Monetary Policy Goals

Central banks typically aim for stable prices (low and stable inflation) and, in the US, maximum employment. When the economy slows, the usual tool is to cut the short-term policy rate, which makes borrowing cheaper and encourages spending. Near zero, that tool runs out, and QE is one alternative.[^boe-qe]

## How QE Works

1. **The central bank creates reserves.** It pays for the bonds with newly created central-bank reserves, not with taxes or borrowing. "Printing money" is the colloquial description; the money is digital.[^boe-qe]
2. **It buys bonds** from investors such as banks and asset managers. The Federal Reserve bought longer-term Treasury securities and agency mortgage-backed securities from late 2008 to October 2014.[^fed-lsap]
3. **Bond prices rise and yields fall**, pushing down long-term borrowing costs such as mortgage rates.[^boe-qe][^fed-lsap]
4. **Portfolio rebalancing.** Sellers of bonds now hold cash and move into other assets, such as corporate bonds and equities, raising their prices and lowering companies' funding costs.[^boe-qe]

The central bank's balance sheet grows, because it holds the bonds as assets and the reserves as liabilities. The earlier version said the main mechanism is banks competing to lend their surplus cash; the central banks' own explanations emphasise lower bond yields and portfolio rebalancing.

## Worked Example: Price and Yield

**Inputs:** a bond paying a fixed coupon of 5 a year, whose price rises from 100 to 120 because of central-bank buying (the Bank of England's illustration).[^boe-qe]

**Step 1: yield at a price of 100.**

$$
\frac{5}{100} = 5\%
$$

**Step 2: yield at a price of 120.**

$$
\frac{5}{120} \approx 4.2\%
$$

The coupon is fixed, so a higher price means a lower yield. Because government bond yields act as benchmarks, lower yields feed through to lower rates on mortgages and other loans.

## Risks and Debates

- **Inflation.** The earlier version listed hyperinflation as a risk. Creating money on this scale can add to inflation, but hyperinflation is an extreme outcome that major QE programmes have not produced; treat this as a debated risk, not an expected effect.
- **Effectiveness.** Injecting reserves does not guarantee that banks lend or households spend. The earlier version cited Japan as an example of QE failing to revive the economy; that claim was not checked here.
- **Asset prices.** Portfolio rebalancing raises asset prices, which benefits people who own assets and may feed [[Market Bubble|bubbles]].
- **Unwinding (quantitative tightening, QT).** Letting bonds mature or selling them shrinks the balance sheet again. The Bank of England bought £895bn of assets in total and began QT in February 2022.[^boe-qe]

See [[10 Year Treasury Note]] for the long-term yields QE targets.
