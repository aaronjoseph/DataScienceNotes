EV/EBITDA compares a company's **enterprise value** (the value of the whole business to both shareholders and lenders) with its [[EBITDA Margin|EBITDA]]. Because both numerator and denominator are before financing, it lets you compare companies with different amounts of debt, which [[Price to Earnings Ratio|P/E]] does not.

## Formulas

**Enterprise value.**

$$
\text{EV} = \text{Market capitalisation} + \text{Debt} - \text{Cash}
$$

Fuller versions also add preferred shares and minority interests.

**The multiple.**

$$
\text{EV/EBITDA} = \frac{\text{EV}}{\text{EBITDA}}
$$

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** market capitalisation 800, debt 300, cash 100 and EBITDA 200.

**Step 1: enterprise value.**

$$
800 + 300 - 100 = 1{,}000
$$

**Step 2: the multiple.**

$$
\frac{1{,}000}{200} = 5
$$

**Step 3: debt repaid from cash flow.** The company uses 100 of operating cash to repay debt, and the market keeps EV at 1,000.

$$
1{,}000 - (200 - 100) = 900
$$

The enterprise is worth the same, but net debt fell from 200 to 100, so the value attributable to shareholders rose from 800 to 900. That is the mechanism behind the commodity argument below.

## Why the Author Favours It for Commodity Stocks

- Debt is one of the most important items to check for commodity companies.
- In an upcycle, operating cash flow improves, highly leveraged producers pay down debt, and EBITDA rises.
- The debt reduction flows to the equity side of the EV equation, which can lift the market capitalisation sharply.
- The analysis should therefore be more balance-sheet-driven than P&L-driven for commodity stocks.

The earlier version also credited the tax shield on interest for boosting earnings in an upcycle. Interest is tax-deductible, but the larger effect in this argument is deleveraging, as the worked example shows.

## Pitfalls

- EBITDA ignores CapEx, which is heavy for commodity producers; cyclical peak EBITDA can make a stock look cheap just before earnings fall.
- Use consistent debt and cash definitions across companies.
