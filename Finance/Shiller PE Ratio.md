The Shiller P/E, or CAPE (cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings) ratio, divides the price by the average of the past ten years of [[Earnings Per Share|earnings]], with both adjusted for inflation. Averaging over a decade smooths out the business cycle, which can make a single year's [[Price to Earnings Ratio|P/E]] misleading. Robert Shiller developed it with John Campbell and publishes US data back to 1871.[^shiller]

## Formula

$$
\text{CAPE} = \frac{\text{Real price}}{\text{Average real EPS over the past 10 years}}
$$

"Real" means adjusted for inflation with a consumer price index. Shiller's data set also includes a total-return version, because companies now return much cash through buybacks rather than dividends, which can bias the standard ratio.[^shiller]

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** an index at 4,000 and inflation-adjusted annual EPS over the past ten years averaging 160.

**Step 1: CAPE.**

$$
\frac{4{,}000}{160} = 25
$$

Investors pay 25 times the decade's average real earnings. This is a single example value; judge it against the market's own long history.

## Interpretation

- CAPE is used to gauge whether a market is expensive or cheap relative to its inflation-adjusted earnings history, and to assess likely returns over 10–20 year horizons: higher CAPE values have been associated with lower subsequent long-term returns.
- It says little about timing over months or even a few years.

## Criticisms

The earlier version recorded these criticisms from an unnamed source. They are arguments, not verified findings, and some are dated:

- Businesses, markets, regulation and accounting rules change over ten years, so old earnings may not be comparable.
- Low interest rates support higher valuations. The original point cited a 40-year secular decline in rates, partly because central banks absorbed fixed-income supply; rates rose again from 2022, so this argument needs updating.
- Demand for stocks has grown (funds, pensions, sovereign funds) while the number of listed US companies has fallen. Not checked here.
- CAPE does not adjust for lower dividend payouts; Shiller's total-return CAPE is designed to address payout-policy changes.[^shiller]
- The long-run average ("the mean") may itself shift, and investors who waited for mean reversion would have missed long expansions.
