The cash flow statement reconciles the cash balance from the start to the end of a period, showing how cash was generated and used. While the [[P&L Statement]] shows whether the company made a profit, the cash flow statement shows whether it generated cash.[^sec-guide] It reports changes over a period, not balances at a point in time.

## Three Sections

1. **Operating activities.** Cash from the core business. Most companies use the **indirect method**: start from net income, add back non-cash items such as depreciation, and adjust for changes in operating assets and liabilities.[^sec-guide] The **direct method** lists cash receipts and payments instead.
2. **Investing activities.** Purchases and sales of long-term assets such as property, plant and equipment, and of investment securities. Buying a machine is an outflow; selling investments is an inflow.[^sec-guide]
3. **Financing activities.** Cash raised by issuing shares or bonds or by borrowing, and cash used to repay loans, buy back shares or pay dividends.[^sec-guide] Where interest paid is classified varies between accounting standards.

$$
\text{Net change in cash} = \text{CFO} + \text{CFI} + \text{CFF}
$$

The net change links the opening and closing cash on the [[Balance Sheet]]. Investors pay most attention to cash from operations (CFO), because it shows whether the business itself produces cash.

## Working-Capital Rule of Thumb (Indirect Method)

The earlier version said that when liabilities rise, cash rises, and when assets rise, cash falls. In the operating section, that holds for **working-capital items**:

- An increase in an operating liability, such as accounts payable, **adds** to CFO: the company has used goods without paying for them yet.
- An increase in a non-cash operating asset, such as receivables or inventory, **reduces** CFO: profit has been booked, but cash has not yet arrived or has been tied up.

Borrowing (a liability) and buying equipment (an asset) follow the same logic, but they appear in the financing and investing sections.

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** net income 100, depreciation 20, receivables up 15, payables up 10, CapEx 50, debt repaid 30.

**Step 1: cash from operations.**

$$
100 + 20 - 15 + 10 = 115
$$

**Step 2: net change in cash.**

$$
115 - 50 - 30 = 35
$$

The company earned 100 of profit but generated 115 of operating cash, spent 50 on assets and 30 on debt repayment, and ended with 35 more cash than it started with.
