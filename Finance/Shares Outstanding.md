Shares outstanding are the company's shares currently held by all its investors: the public, institutional investors, and company officers and insiders (including restricted shares). Shares the company has bought back and holds as treasury stock are not outstanding. They are reported in the equity section of the balance sheet, often under a heading such as capital stock.

## Why the Count Changes

- **Share issues** to raise equity capital, or through employee stock options, increase it.
- **Buybacks** reduce it.
- **[[Stock Split|Stock splits]]** multiply it without changing the company's value.

## Formula

$$
\text{Shares outstanding} = \text{Shares issued} - \text{Treasury shares}
$$

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** 1,000 shares issued, of which the company has bought back 50; net income of 475.

**Step 1: shares outstanding.**

$$
1{,}000 - 50 = 950
$$

**Step 2: EPS on the outstanding shares.**

$$
\frac{475}{950} = 0.5
$$

The buyback lifted EPS from 0.475 to 0.5 without any change in profit, which is why the share count matters when reading [[Earnings Per Share]].
