Depreciation and amortisation spread the cost of a long-lived asset over the years in which it is used, instead of expensing it all at purchase. Depreciation applies to tangible fixed assets; amortisation applies to intangible assets. Both are non-cash expenses on the [[P&L Statement]]: the cash left when the asset was bought.[^sec-guide] They also reduce taxable profit, although tax depreciation rules can differ from the accounting rules.

## Depreciation

Depreciation allocates the cost of a tangible fixed asset over its useful life, reflecting how much of it is used up. Typical depreciable assets are:

- Buildings
- Plant, machinery and equipment
- Office furniture
- Vehicles

**Land is normally not depreciated**, because it does not wear out; the earlier version of this note listed it.

## Amortisation

Amortisation allocates the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life. Examples include:

- Patents and trademarks
- Copyrights and proprietary processes
- Franchise agreements
- Costs of issuing bonds, spread over the life of the debt

## Straight-Line Method

$$
\text{Annual charge} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual value}}{\text{Useful life}}
$$

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** a machine costing 10,000 with a residual value of 1,000 and a 5-year life.

**Step 1: annual depreciation.**

$$
\frac{10{,}000 - 1{,}000}{5} = 1{,}800
$$

**Step 2: net book value after 2 years.**

$$
10{,}000 - 2 \times 1{,}800 = 6{,}400
$$

The P&L shows 1,800 of expense each year, while the balance sheet carries the machine at cost less accumulated depreciation; see [[Non Current Asset]].

## Interpretation

**Author's heuristic:** depreciation eats into profits, so compare it with [[Gross Profit]]. A high and rising ratio of depreciation to gross profit suggests a capital-intensive business that must keep reinvesting, which can mean more borrowing and lower earnings.
