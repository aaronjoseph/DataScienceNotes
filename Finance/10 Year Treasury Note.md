The 10-year Treasury note is a debt obligation of the United States government that matures 10 years after issue. It pays a fixed rate of interest every six months and repays the face value at maturity. Treasury sells notes with terms of 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years; the rate is fixed at auction and does not change over the life of the note.[^td-notes]

## Key Facts

- **Coupon:** fixed at auction, paid every six months until maturity.[^td-notes]
- **Auctions:** new 10-year notes are auctioned in February, May, August and November, with reopenings in other months.[^td-notes]
- **Minimum purchase:** $100, in $100 increments, bought through TreasuryDirect or a broker.[^td-notes]
- **Taxes:** interest is subject to US federal income tax but exempt from state and local income tax.[^td-notes]

## Coupon versus Yield

The coupon is the fixed interest rate on the face value. The **yield** is the return implied by the market price. When the price rises above face value, the yield falls below the coupon, and vice versa. For example, TreasuryDirect listed a 10-year note issued on 15 September 2026 with a 4.625% rate at a price of $98.36 per $100, so its yield at issue was above 4.625%.[^td-notes]

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** a $1,000 note with a 4% annual coupon, held to maturity.

**Step 1: each semi-annual payment.**

$$
\frac{0.04 \times 1{,}000}{2} = 20
$$

**Step 2: total interest over 10 years** (20 payments).

$$
20 \times 20 = 400
$$

The holder receives $400 of interest plus the $1,000 face value at maturity. If market rates rise after purchase, the note's price falls, but a holder who keeps it to maturity still receives these fixed amounts.

## Why It Matters

The 10-year yield is widely watched as a benchmark for longer-term borrowing costs, such as mortgage rates, and as the risk-free rate in many valuations; see [[Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)]]. Central-bank bond purchases also work through these longer-term yields; see [[Quantitative Easing]].
