Gross profit is what remains of revenue after the direct costs of producing the goods or services sold. It does not yet deduct selling and administrative costs, research, depreciation of non-production assets, interest or tax.[^sec-guide]

## Formulas

**Gross profit.**

$$
\text{Gross profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{COGS}
$$

**Gross profit margin.**

$$
\text{Gross profit margin} = \frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Revenue}}
$$

See [[Revenue]] and [[Cost of Goods Sold]].

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** revenue 10,000 and COGS 3,000.

**Step 1: gross profit.**

$$
10{,}000 - 3{,}000 = 7{,}000
$$

**Step 2: margin.**

$$
\frac{7{,}000}{10{,}000} = 70\%
$$

The company keeps 70 of every 100 of sales after production costs. That clears the author's 20% threshold in the [[Stock Selection Checklist]] easily.

## Interpretation

- **Author's heuristic:** companies with a consistently high gross margin tend to have a durable competitive advantage, such as pricing power.
- A high gross margin can still leave little profit if [[Operating Expense|operating expenses]] are high: research ([[R&D Costs]]), [[Selling, General and Administrative Expense|selling and administration]], or interest on heavy debt.
- Piotroski's F-score awards a point when the gross margin improves year on year; see [[Piotroski Score]].
