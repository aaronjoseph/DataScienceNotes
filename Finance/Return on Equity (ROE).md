Return on equity (ROE) measures the profit a company generates for each unit of shareholders' capital. It shows how efficiently the company turns shareholders' money into profit; other things equal, higher is better. It is one of the [[Profitability Ratios]].

## Formula

$$
\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Shareholders' equity}} \times 100\%
$$

## The Flaw: Debt Inflates ROE

More debt means less equity for the same assets, so ROE rises even if the business is no better. A high ROE funded by expensive debt is not a good sign. The **DuPont identity** exposes this by splitting ROE into three factors:

$$
\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Net sales}} \times \frac{\text{Net sales}}{\text{Average total assets}} \times \frac{\text{Average total assets}}{\text{Shareholders' equity}}
$$

The factors are the net profit margin, [[Total Assets Turnover|asset turnover]] and [[Financial Leverage Ratio|financial leverage]]. The sales and asset terms cancel, so the product equals the simple formula; the breakdown shows **why** ROE is high.

## Worked Example

**Inputs:** net profit 975, net sales 10,000, average total assets 12,000 and equity 6,000.

**Step 1: simple ROE.**

$$
\frac{975}{6{,}000} = 16.25\%
$$

**Step 2: net profit margin.**

$$
\frac{975}{10{,}000} = 9.75\%
$$

**Step 3: asset turnover.**

$$
\frac{10{,}000}{12{,}000} \approx 0.833
$$

**Step 4: financial leverage.**

$$
\frac{12{,}000}{6{,}000} = 2
$$

**Step 5: DuPont product.**

$$
0.0975 \times 0.833 \times 2 \approx 16.25\%
$$

Both routes give 16.25%. Leverage doubles the return that the assets alone produce, so if the company halved its equity by borrowing, ROE would double without any improvement in the business. The author's checklist asks for ROE above 25% but always together with a debt check; see [[Stock Selection Checklist]].
